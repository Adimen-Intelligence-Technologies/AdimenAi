# Process automation: what it is and how to apply it

**** Process automation means putting software to work on the tasks a person does over and over again: logging an order, matching an invoice with its delivery note, flagging that material is running low, putting together Monday's report. It is not a robot in the factory: it is repeated steps running on their own, with your own rules, without anyone getting bored or making a typo because they are tired. In this post we explain it without jargon, with examples you will find in any business, and we tell you why at **Adimen** we specialise in automating **industrial processes**: because that is where an hour saved is felt in the warehouse, on the line and on the invoice.

## A process is just a chain of steps

Before talking about technology, it is worth understanding what a process is, because it sounds more complicated than it really is.

A process is a series of steps repeated to get the same result every time. Think about a customer order: the request comes in → we check whether the material is in stock → it is prepared → it is invoiced → the transport is notified. You write it down in a manual and it works the same way every day.

So far, nothing unusual. The detail is that **each step is done by someone**, and almost always the same person, day after day. And as soon as a chain of steps is repeated hundreds of times a month, a very reasonable question comes up: why can't the system do it?

That question is, in essence, the whole of process automation.

## What process automation actually is

Process automation is the way of having a program carry out your company's repetitive tasks on its own, applying the rules you define and connecting to the software you already use.

It helps to explain it with a comparison, because that is easier to grasp:

> A good employee takes two days to learn a process and then runs it in three hours. A well-automated process runs in seconds, every day, without breaks and without typing mistakes.

The difference is not that the machine is smarter than the person. It is that **the system does not get tired, does not get distracted by a phone and does not change from one day to the next**.

When someone says "I want to automate", they often picture a movie factory: robotic arms, robots painting cars. That kind of automation exists and makes sense on a production line, but it is the most expensive and the one that demands the biggest investment. There is another kind, far cheaper and much faster to deploy, which works with the software you already have: the ERP, email, the spreadsheet, the invoicing tool.

### Three levels, from the simplest to the most powerful

**1. Fixed rules.** The system does one thing when something else happens. *"If a PDF arrives with the supplier's name in the subject line, file it in the invoices folder and let admin know."* This is called RPA (robotic process automation) and it covers most copying, moving, renaming and form-filling work.

**2. Reading documents.** AI reads a scanned delivery note, a PDF invoice or a handwritten email, pulls out the product, quantity and price, and passes it to the next step. Nobody has to type the information into a form any more.

**3. Making decisions.** The system reviews what it has read, compares it with what it should be, spots what does not add up and alerts the person who decides. The human does not disappear from the process: they stop typing and start deciding. This is what we call AI automations at Adimen.

## Examples of automation you will see within a week

You do not need a huge factory to automate. These are real processes, the kind that show up in almost any company, with what happens today and what changes once they are automated.

| Process | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Orders arriving by email | The admin forwards 40 PDFs, prints them and types them one by one into the ERP: 3 hours | The PDFs go to an inbox, the system reads them and logs the order. 3 hours → 3 minutes |
| Invoices and delivery notes | They are matched by hand to see if they match; discrepancies surface at month end | Every invoice is checked against its delivery note and mismatches pop up on their own |
| Quality control | Someone inspects the parts and writes them in a notebook; the customer finds the defect | A camera on the line flags the faulty part and stops the process |
| Maintenance | When a machine breaks down, the spare parts have already been ordered | The machine warns before the breakdown and raises the work order |
| Stock and replenishment | Someone goes through the list by hand and remembers to order on Monday | When material drops below the minimum, the purchase order goes out on its own |
| Batch traceability | Each batch record is filled in by hand, with a pen, in a folder | Every batch carries its own record (expiry, allergens, temperature), updated on the spot |
| Email management | The inbox is chaos: the invoice, the breakdown and the angry customer in the same pile | Every email is sorted, answered or escalated to the right person |
| Weekly reports | On Monday, someone copies data into a spreadsheet for the manager | The summary arrives on its own by email or mobile, every Monday |
| Shifts and access control | Timetables and access on lists and paper | Access is logged automatically and the rota updates itself |
| Customer service | The same questions answered by hand on WhatsApp all day | An assistant answers the repetitive stuff around the clock and hands the important stuff to a person |

All of these cases have something in common: **the input is digital, the rule is clear and the result can be checked**. If a process meets those three conditions, it is a good candidate for automation.

## What process automation is not

Honesty is worth more than promises, so it is worth clarifying five things.

**It does not mean sacking your team.** What disappears is the repetitive, boring work. Your people move on to handling exceptions, looking after customers properly and selling. In practice, companies that automate redeploy their staff, they do not let them go.

**It is not a one-year project.** A single process goes live in 2-8 weeks, depending on how much it touches. A full project with several connected processes, in 4-8 months.

**It is not about automating a broken process.** If orders are lost because every customer emails a different address, automating that only makes the mess move faster. You fix the process first; then you automate it.

**It does not suit everything.** Decisions that need judgement, dealing with an angry customer and creativity stay with people. Automation takes care of the mechanical work, not the judgement.

**It does not mean replacing your machines or your ERP.** It connects to what you already have: SAP, Dynamics, Holded, Sage, spreadsheets, email and internal messaging.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does automating mean sacking people?**
No. What gets removed is the repetitive work. Your team moves on to higher-value tasks: analysis, handling exceptions, looking after people. The usual outcome is redeployment, not redundancies.

**How long until I see the first results?**
The first automations usually start delivering within 2-4 weeks. The full return on investment shows up between 4 and 12 months, depending on the process.

**Does it work with my ERP?**
Yes. We work with SAP, Dynamics AX, Holded, Sage and any system with an API or database access. If your ERP is old, we integrate through that route.

**What if my data is a mess?**
Diagnosing data quality is part of the initial audit. If it is not clean, we clean it first or design the automation to tolerate the noise.

**Can I automate a process a person is involved in?**
Yes, and it is the most common case. The person stops doing the mechanical step and moves on to resolving what the system flags as an exception. That is much easier to accept than an all-or-nothing change.

**Do I need to know about technology?**
No. We handle the development and the integration. Your team only needs to know how to use the result, and we design it to be as simple as possible.

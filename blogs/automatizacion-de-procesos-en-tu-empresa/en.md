# Process automation: how to apply it in your company

**** Process automation is not decided in a brainstorming meeting: it is applied in steps, and in the right order. ## First things first: it almost never fails because of technology

The number one reason an automation does not work is not the software, not the budget, and not a lack of technology. It is that **the wrong process has been automated**, or the right process has been documented by someone who does not do it.

So the real work does not start when you write code. It starts earlier, and it looks more like a conversation than a software project. And in industrial companies there is one extra ingredient that other sectors do not have: **the process is not in a manual, it is in the plant, in the machine and in the head of the person who has been doing it for 12 years**.

## Step 1 · Photograph how your company works today

Sit down with the three or four people who know the process best and walk the real flow with them. Not to interview them, but to watch: "show me how you do it, with a real case from last week".

Three things come out of that meeting that no manual will give you:

- **How much time it really takes** at each step (not what you think, what you time).
- **Where the information gets lost**: which email, which folder, which notebook, which head.
- **Which variations exist**: orders that arrive as PDFs, others as spreadsheets, others over the phone.

The result is a one-page map. That document drives everything else, so do not skip it.

## Step 2 · Decide where to start (and not where it is easiest to start)

Automating is the easy part; choosing well is the real work. The temptation is to start with the simplest process, and it is usually a mistake: if you automate something that was not hurting, you will not notice anything.

Filter your processes with these four questions:

1. Does it happen several times a week, or every day?
2. Is it always done the same way, following rules you could write in a sentence?
3. Does it eat hours of someone's time, or produce errors that cost money?
4. Does the information go in and come out digitally (email, PDF, spreadsheet, ERP)?

If you answer "yes" to three of the four, you have a candidate. If you answer "no" to two or more, leave it for later.

A couple of real examples of where an industrial company starts:

| Candidate process | Why it qualifies | Typical gain |
|---|---|---|
| Logging orders that arrive by email | Daily, the rule is fixed, they are PDFs | 2-3 hours/day of admin staff |
| Matching invoices with delivery notes | Happens at every close, the mismatch shows up late | No unmatched invoices at month end |
| Flagging missing material | Very clear rule (stock below the minimum) and 100% digital | Production stops avoided |

## Step 3 · Document the "as is" the way it is really done

This is the step most people skip, and the one that avoids the most disappointments.

The process you are going to automate **is not the one in the manual**: it is the one your staff member does with the mouse, in the order they do it, skipping two steps nobody considers important and stopping to ask a colleague something. If you document the "nice" process, you will automate a process that does not exist.

How to document it properly, without jargon:

- Ask the person who does it to **show you with a real case** (a PDF, an email, an actual invoice).
- Record or note **screenshots** of each step and of the exact field in the ERP where the data ends up.
- Write down the **exceptions**: what happens when the client sends the order in another format, or when the quantity does not add up.

That last point is what separates a project that works from one that breaks in the second week.

## Step 4 · Design the pilot for four weeks

No "automate the whole back office". The first project has to be **small, measurable and closable in a month**. At Adimen we always work that way:

1. **What goes in** (an email, a PDF, a folder) and **what comes out** (a row in the ERP, an alert, a file).
2. **What the system does** step by step, and **what a person does** (usually the exceptions and the odd cases).
3. **What happens if it fails**: if the system does not understand an order, it does not hang in silence: it alerts someone with the error context.
4. **What we measure** when the four weeks are up (we look at it in step 6).

With that framing, a pilot on a straightforward process is usually live in 2-4 weeks.

## Step 5 · Choose how to build it (and why not "with AI" by default)

Not everything needs artificial intelligence, and using AI where it is not needed costs more and fails more. The criterion is simple:

- **If the document always has the same format** → fixed rules. An order that always arrives as a PDF with the same columns is automated with a rule, which is cheaper, faster and more predictable.
- **If the format changes or the content has to be understood** (scanned invoices, handwritten emails, product photos) → AI. There the system reads, extracts and decides.
- **If the decision matters and is ambiguous** → the person. The AI prepares, alerts and sorts; the person decides and justifies.

And one decision worth making early: **you connect to the ERP, you do not replace it**. We work with SAP, Dynamics AX, Holded, Sage and with any system we can reach. Replacing the ERP to automate a process is starting at the wrong end of the tunnel.

## Step 6 · Measure with three numbers, not fifteen

When the pilot closes, look at three things. Only three:

| Indicator | Before | After (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Time per order | 12 minutes | 1 minute |
| Typing errors per month | 40 | 2 |
| Stock or customer incidents | 5 | 1 |

If you can only watch one, watch the **time per task**: it is the number management understands without needing anything explained.

And write down the "before" and "after" picture: that is what turns an investment into an argument for the next project.

## Step 7 · Scale up and leave the process better than you found it

A pilot that works is only the beginning. So that it does not fall apart after three months:

- **Document** how the automation works (one page: what it does, what it does not, who to alert).
- **Assign an owner for the process**: someone on your team who knows what to do when something goes wrong. Without that person, the automation ages and nobody dares touch it.
- **Train the team** in using it, not just at launch.
- **Repeat the pattern**: the second process usually costs half as much as the first, because the groundwork is already there.

## Seven mistakes that ruin an automation

1. **Automating a broken process.** If the order is lost because every client emails a different address, automating it only makes the mess move faster.
2. **Starting with the technology.** Buying a tool before knowing which problem you are solving is the fastest way to not solve it.
3. **Automating without measuring the "before".** If you do not know what it cost, you cannot show you have won.
4. **Not deciding who owns the exceptions.** Someone has to pick up the phone when the system does not understand a case.
5. **Leaving users out.** Whoever does the process every day knows where the shortcut is: if they are not part of the design, they will go around it.
6. **Integrating only through the screen.** Automating by clicking where a person used to click works until the day the screen changes and the process stops dead.
7. **Wanting to automate everything at once.** One big project does not scale: you start with one, you prove it and you add the next.

## Checklist: 10 questions before automating a process

Answer honestly. If six or more have a clear answer, you already have a candidate project:

1. How often does it repeat?
2. Who does it and how long does it take them?
3. Is it always done with the same rules?
4. What happens when it does not fit? Who handles that today?
5. How many errors does it produce a month?
6. Where does the information come from and where does the result go?
7. Which systems would it have to touch (ERP, spreadsheet, email, POS)?
8. Is there any reason only one particular person can do it?
9. What would you measure to prove it was a success?
10. Who would own the process once it is automated?
